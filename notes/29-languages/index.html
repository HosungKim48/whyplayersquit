Turning reviews in 29 languages into one dataset for $4

Notes · Nov 19, 2026 · Hosung Kim

How 207,494 Steam reviews became one labeled table, without a translation step, for under four dollars in model costs.

WARDOGS players review in a lot of languages. Of the 56,187 negative reviews I ended up labeling, about half were in English, a quarter in Simplified Chinese, and the rest spread across Russian, German, Korean, Traditional Chinese, Portuguese and 22 more. Any analysis that only reads English misses a big part of the story. Here is how the dataset came together.

Collect everything

Steam has a public reviews API that needs no key. A few weeks before I started, Steam retired the old endpoint, so the collector uses the newer one. It pages through reviews 100 at a time, waits one second between requests, and backs off if rate-limited. For WARDOGS that meant 2,077 pages and 207,494 reviews from launch day, Sep 10, to Nov 19, 2026.

Each review comes with the text, the language, whether it was positive, and two playtime numbers: hours at the time of the review and hours now. Those two numbers are what make quit analysis possible.

Filter cheaply before spending anything

Most of the work is free. Code kept only negative reviews (61,836), then dropped very short ones. The rule is 20 characters, except 8 for Chinese, Japanese and Korean, where a short string carries a full sentence. That left 56,196 reviews. Nothing here needs a model; it's just a few lines of pandas.

Label in the original language

The usual approach would be to translate everything into English and then classify it. I skipped translation. The model reads each review in its original language and answers in English, using the same 12 category codes for everyone. That removes a whole step, and a source of error: translation flattens the slang and in-game shorthand players use.

It also keeps categories consistent across languages, which matters because languages tell different stories. Among Simplified Chinese negative reviews, the ping cap was the main reason in 18.6%. Among English ones, the most common main reason was balance, at 16.9%. A study in one language would get a different "top complaint" depending on which one it picked.

Make it cheap: batches and caching

Two features kept the cost tiny.

  • Batch processing. Instead of sending reviews one by one and waiting, I submitted them as batch jobs that finish within hours at a lower price.
  • Prompt caching. Every request starts with the same 6,432-token codebook. With caching, the model reads it from a cache instead of paying full price for it every time. Across the full run, cached input made up the vast majority of tokens.

The labeling run cost $3.50 for 56,187 labeled reviews. The 50-review pilot cost under a cent, and the first report draft about 14 cents. All model costs together came to under $4.

Before each paid run I wrote down the review count, estimated tokens and estimated cost, and only ran it once those were approved. Partly that's discipline. Mostly it's because a wrong setting at 56,187 reviews is expensive, while at 50 it's a lesson.

What the money didn't buy

Cheap labeling isn't the same as correct labeling. The model was about 88% accurate in my spot check, and I explain how I checked in a separate note. Nine reviews failed to label and were dropped. And a dataset of negative reviews only represents people who chose to write one.

But the economics change what's possible. Reading 56,187 reviews in 29 languages by hand would take a team weeks. Here, the expensive part was the thinking: reading 300 reviews myself, writing the codebook, and deciding which questions the data could actually answer.

Numbers come from the WARDOGS project and its method. Data covers only people who wrote negative reviews.
